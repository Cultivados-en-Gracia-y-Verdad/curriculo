# A Question Worth Asking

Suppose someone asked:

> What was Jesus actually doing with the disciples?

Looking only at the narratives, we might answer:

-   calling them,
-   bringing them with Him,
-   exposing them to Himself,
-   teaching them,
-   correcting them,
-   involving them,
-   sending them,
-   bringing them back,
-   continuing with them.

Notice that all of those activities revolve around Jesus.

The process does not appear separable from Him.

If the Logos is not merely a New Testament reality, then perhaps the continuity is not:

> Elijah resembles Jesus.

or

> Joshua resembles Jesus.

But rather:

> People in every age are ultimately being formed by God through His self-revelation.

The form changes.

##### The center does not.

# A Very Important Question

What if the issue is not discipleship itself?

What if the issue is the object of discipleship?

Because Scripture clearly contains:

-   Moses
-   Elijah
-   John
-   Paul

all exercising profound influence on people.

The problem never seems to be influence.

The problem appears when the influence terminates on the influencer.

# A Possible Test

Let's test every major figure we've studied.

### Abraham
Who is Abraham pointing toward?
Ultimately God.

### Moses
Who is Moses pointing toward?
Ultimately God.

### Elijah
Who is Elijah pointing toward?
Ultimately Yahweh.

### John
Who is John pointing toward?
Jesus.

### Paul
Who is Paul pointing toward?
Christ.
Now ask:

### Jesus
Who is Jesus pointing toward?
This is where things become very interesting.
Because Jesus certainly points people to the Father.
Yet He also says:

> Follow Me.

> Come to Me.

> Abide in Me.

> Believe in Me.

> My disciples.

No prophet talks like that.

No apostle talks like that.

No Old Testament figure talks like that.

This may be the deepest distinction we've found so far.

Every other servant appears to function as a witness.

Jesus functions as the One to whom the witnesses testify.

If that observation survives further testing, it could radically reshape how we think about discipleship.

Because then the question becomes:

> Are Christians called to make disciples for themselves?

Or

> Are Christians called to function like Moses, John, and Paul—bringing people to Christ?

And so far, the evidence seems to be leaning strongly toward the second option.

# Things the Text Seems to Be Showing Repeatedly

## 1. God Initiates

This appears everywhere.

-   Abraham
-   Moses
-   Samuel
-   Prophets
-   Disciples
-   Paul

The movement begins with God's action, not man's.

## 2. The Center Is Consistently God / Christ

This may be the strongest observation so far.

### OT
-   Abraham is not the destination.
-   Moses is not the destination.
-   Elijah is not the destination.

### NT
-   John points to Jesus.
-   Paul points to Christ.
-   Jesus receives the allegiance that others redirect.

This observation has survived every stage of the study.

## 3. Human Instruments Matter

The text does not eliminate them.

-   Moses matters.
-   Elijah matters.
-   Eli matters.
-   John matters.
-   Paul matters.

The question is not whether they matter.

The question is what role they play.

## 4. Scripture Repeatedly Describes Formation

We have seen:

-   hearing
-   learning
-   following
-   remaining
-   walking
-   keeping
-   imitating
-   continuing

Those verbs appear across both Testaments.

## 5. Formation Is Usually Relational

Not merely informational.

God does not simply hand Abraham a textbook.

God walks Abraham through a life.

Likewise:

-   Israel in the wilderness
-   disciples with Jesus
-   churches under apostolic care

There is an ongoing relationship.
