# Lesson 1 — Why Structure Matters

## The Problem Most Readers Face

When people read a passage of Scripture, they usually ask:

> *What does this mean?*

But that question often comes **too early**.

Before we can understand meaning, we must first understand **how the author built the paragraph**.

Authors communicate purpose through **structure**.

If we skip structure, we often misunderstand the author's intention.

------

## The Hidden Architecture of a Paragraph

Every paragraph contains an internal structure.

Some statements are **primary**.
 Others **explain** those statements.

Some clauses give **reasons**.
 Others express **conditions**, **results**, or **contrasts**.

The author's purpose becomes clear when we see **how these parts work together**.

------

## The Process of Discovery

Instead of jumping directly to interpretation, we will follow a simple process of observation.

The process moves step-by-step from the smallest elements of the text toward the author's purpose.

```
WORDS → VERBS → CLAUSES → CONNECTIONS → STRUCTURE → SPINE → PURPOSE
```

This progression allows the reader to move from **observation** to **understanding**.

------

## Why This Method Works

This method protects the reader from reading personal ideas into the text.

Instead of asking immediately, *“What does this mean?”*, we first ask:

- What actions are present?
- What clauses express those actions?
- How are those clauses connected?
- What structure emerges from those connections?

When the structure becomes visible, the author's purpose often becomes **obvious**.

------

## The Goal of the Course

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

- recognize the structure of a paragraph
- identify how clauses relate to one another
- locate the paragraph’s central statement
- state the author's purpose clearly and accurately

This process will help students read Scripture with greater clarity and confidence.

------

# Lesson 2 — Step 1: Copy the Paragraph

Before analyzing a passage, we must first prepare the text so that its structure can be observed.

This preparation step may seem simple, but it is essential.

------

## Why We Begin with the Paragraph

The paragraph is one of the most important units of meaning in written communication.

Authors normally develop **one primary idea per paragraph**.

If we divide a passage too early into verses or sentences, we may miss how those thoughts connect.

For this reason, we begin by placing the entire paragraph into a working document.

------

## Preparing the Text

Follow these steps:

1. Copy the paragraph into a text editor.
2. Remove titles or section headings.
3. Combine the verses into a single paragraph.
4. Keep verse numbers only if they help orientation.

Example:

```
And I, brothers, could not speak to you as to spiritual men,
but as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.
I gave you milk to drink, not solid food;
for you were not yet able.
```

This becomes the **reference text**.

## Create a Working Copy

Next, duplicate the paragraph beneath the original.

The original text remains untouched.
 The copied paragraph will become the **working text** where analysis occurs.

Keeping the original paragraph visible allows students to:

- restart the process if necessary
- compare stages of analysis
- avoid accidentally changing the text

# Lesson 3 — Step 2: Mark the Finite Verbs

## Why We Begin with Verbs

After preparing the paragraph, the next step is to locate the **finite verbs**.

This step is critical because **verbs anchor clauses**.

A clause normally exists wherever a **finite verb expresses an action or state**. By identifying these verbs, we begin to uncover the **thought units** that make up the paragraph.

In other words, verbs help us discover where the author is **making assertions**.

------

## What Is a Finite Verb?

A finite verb is a verb that is limited by **person, number, and tense**. It functions as the main verb of a clause.

Finite verbs typically express:

- an action
- a condition
- a state of being

Examples:

- *gave*
- *were*
- *walk*
- *confess*
- *is*

Non-finite forms such as infinitives or participles do **not usually anchor clauses**.

Examples:

- *to speak*
- *walking*
- *having been*

These forms may add information, but they usually do not create a new clause.

------

## Marking the Verbs

To make verbs easy to identify, we will mark them using a simple visual indicator.

Use this format:

```
==verb==
```

Example:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men,
but as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.
I ==gave== you milk to drink, not solid food;
for you ==were== not yet able.
```

This marking system helps the verbs stand out clearly from the rest of the text.

------

## What Students Should Notice

After marking the verbs, students should pause and observe:

- How many verbs appear in the paragraph?
- Where are they located?
- Which statements seem to revolve around them?

Each verb signals a possible **clause anchor**.

This observation prepares us for the next step, where we will separate the paragraph into **individual clauses**.

------

## A Helpful Insight

At this stage students often begin to notice something important.

A paragraph that first appeared as a single block of text now begins to reveal **multiple actions and assertions**.

For example, in the paragraph above we observe three verbs:

- *speak*
- *gave*
- *were*

These verbs suggest that the paragraph contains **multiple thought units**, not just one long statement.

------

## Why This Step Matters

Marking the verbs allows students to see the **framework of the paragraph**.

Before identifying clauses or connections, we must first recognize the **actions and assertions** being made.

Once the verbs are visible, the next step will be to identify **connectors**, which reveal how these clauses relate to one another.

------

# What Comes Next

In the next lesson we will identify the **connectors** within the paragraph.

Connectors reveal the **logical relationships** between clauses, such as:

- reason
- contrast
- condition
- result

Once verbs and connectors are visible, the paragraph’s **structure begins to emerge**.

# Lesson 4 — Step 3: Identify the Connectors

## Why Connectors Matter

After identifying the verbs, the next step is to locate the **connectors** within the paragraph.

Connectors are words that show how one clause relates to another. They reveal the **logical relationships** between ideas.

Authors use connectors to indicate relationships such as:

- contrast
- reason
- condition
- result
- time
- addition

When we identify these connectors, we begin to see **how the author’s thoughts are connected**.

------

## What Connectors Do

Connectors do not usually create clauses themselves. Instead, they help us understand **how clauses interact**.

For example, consider the difference between these two statements:

```
I gave you milk.
You were not able.
```

These two statements exist side by side, but their relationship is unclear.

Now consider the same statements connected with a logical word:

```
I gave you milk,
for you were not able.
```

The connector **for** reveals the relationship.

Now we understand that the second statement provides the **reason** for the first.

------

## Recognizing Connectors

Many connectors appear frequently in Scripture. Some common examples include:

| Connector | Function          |
| --------- | ----------------- |
| for       | reason            |
| because   | reason            |
| but       | contrast          |
| if        | condition         |
| when      | time              |
| and       | addition          |
| so that   | purpose or result |

These words signal that the author is **linking one idea to another**.

------

## Marking the Connectors

To make connectors visible in the text, we will mark them using a simple format:

```
(connector → FUNCTION)
```

Example:

```
(for → REASON)
(but → CONTRAST)
(if → CONDITION)
(and → ADDITION)
```

This marking system shows both the **connector itself** and the **relationship it introduces**.

------

## Example

Consider the following paragraph with verbs already marked:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men,
but as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.
I ==gave== you milk to drink, not solid food;
for you ==were== not yet able.
```

Now we identify the connectors:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men,
(but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.

I ==gave== you milk to drink, not solid food;
(for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able.
```

Notice that the connectors reveal two relationships:

- **but** introduces contrast
- **for** introduces reason

# Lesson 5 — Step 4: Separate the Clauses

## Why We Separate Clauses

After identifying the verbs and connectors, the next step is to separate the paragraph into **individual clauses**.

A clause is a group of words built around a **finite verb** that expresses a thought.

When a paragraph is written normally, several clauses may appear together within the same sentence. Separating them allows us to observe each **thought unit** more clearly.

This step helps transform a paragraph from a continuous block of text into a set of **distinct statements**.

------

## Using the Verbs as Anchors

The verbs identified in the previous step help us locate the clauses.

Each clause normally contains **one finite verb** that serves as the anchor of that thought.

For example, in the following paragraph the verbs have already been marked:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men,
(but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.
I ==gave== you milk to drink, not solid food;
(for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able.
```

These verbs indicate where the clauses appear.

------

## Placing Each Clause on Its Own Line

To make the clauses visible, we place each clause on a **separate line**.

At this stage we do **not indent anything yet**. The goal is simply to isolate each clause.

Example:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men

(but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh

I ==gave== you milk to drink

(for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

Now the paragraph is no longer hidden inside sentences. Each clause can be examined individually.

------

## What Students Should Notice

After separating the clauses, students should observe:

- how many clauses appear in the paragraph
- which clauses contain the verbs previously identified
- which clauses begin with connectors

These observations will prepare the student for the next step.

------

## A Helpful Reminder

At this stage we are still **not interpreting the paragraph**.

We are only revealing the **units of thought** that the author has written.

The structure of the paragraph is still flat because we have not yet determined which clauses are **primary** and which are **dependent**.

That distinction will appear in the next step.

# Lesson 6 — Step 5: Indent Dependent Clauses

## Why We Indent Clauses

After separating the clauses, the paragraph still appears as a list of statements. However, not all clauses carry the same weight.

Some clauses express the **main assertions** of the paragraph.
 Other clauses exist to **explain, support, or qualify** those assertions.

Indentation allows us to show this difference visually.

By placing supporting clauses beneath the clauses they explain, we reveal the **hierarchical structure** of the paragraph.

------

## Recognizing Dependent Clauses

A dependent clause is a clause that relies on another clause for its full meaning.

These clauses often appear with connectors such as:

- **for** (reason)
- **but** (contrast)
- **if** (condition)
- **so that** (purpose)
- **when** (time)

These connectors signal that the clause is **supporting or qualifying another statement**.

------

## Using Connectors as Clues

The connectors we identified earlier often help determine where a clause belongs.

For example, a clause beginning with **for** frequently provides the **reason** for the clause before it.

Consider the following clauses:

```
I ==gave== you milk to drink

(for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

The connector **for** indicates that the second clause explains the first.

Therefore, the second clause should be placed beneath the first.

------

## Indenting the Clauses

Now we arrange the clauses to reflect their relationships.

Example:

```
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you as to spiritual men
    (but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh

I ==gave== you milk to drink
    (for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

Notice that the supporting clauses are placed beneath the clauses they relate to.

This indentation reveals two main statements:

- I could not speak to you as to spiritual men
- I gave you milk to drink

The other clauses explain or qualify these statements.

------

## What Students Should Observe

Once the clauses are indented, students should begin to notice:

- which clauses appear to be **central**
- which clauses appear to **support or explain** those statements
- how the paragraph begins to display a **visual structure**

This visual arrangement helps students recognize the **logical flow of the author's thought**.

------

## A Helpful Reminder

At this stage we are not adding new ideas to the paragraph.

We are simply arranging the clauses in a way that makes the author's structure **visible**.

Different readers should arrive at **very similar structures** because the relationships between clauses are already present in the text.

# Lesson 8 — Step 7: Add Phrases

## Why We Add Phrases Last

During the earlier steps of this method, we intentionally ignored many smaller parts of the sentence. This allowed us to focus first on the **core structure of the paragraph**.

Now that the clauses and their relationships are visible, we can safely restore the remaining elements of the text.

These elements are called **phrases**.

Phrases add detail to a clause, but they usually do not determine the overall structure of the paragraph.

For this reason, phrases are added **after the clause structure has been established**.

------

## What Is a Phrase?

A phrase is a group of words that adds information but does **not contain its own finite verb**.

Phrases often describe:

- location
- manner
- object
- comparison
- description

Examples include:

- *to you*
- *as to spiritual men*
- *not solid food*
- *in Christ*

These phrases modify the meaning of the clause, but they do not normally create new clauses.

------

## Restoring the Phrases

When restoring phrases, we place them beneath the clause they modify.

Consider the structure that was developed in the previous lesson:

```
[STATEMENT]
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you
    (but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh

[STATEMENT]
I ==gave== you milk to drink
    (for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

Now we restore the phrases that belong to those clauses.

Example:

```
[STATEMENT]
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you
    as to spiritual men
    (but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh
        as to infants in Christ

[STATEMENT]
I ==gave== you milk to drink
    not solid food
    (for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

Notice that the phrases are placed under the clause they modify.

The clause structure remains unchanged, but the paragraph now contains its full detail.

------

## What Students Should Notice

After phrases are added, students should observe that:

- the paragraph structure remains visible
- supporting clauses are still easy to identify
- the additional phrases simply expand the meaning of the clauses

Because we waited to add phrases until this step, the paragraph remains **clear and organized**.

------

## A Helpful Insight

Students often discover that many phrases provide helpful **explanatory detail**, but they rarely determine the paragraph's main structure.

This is why phrases are handled last.

By first identifying verbs, clauses, connectors, and indentation, we uncover the **framework of the paragraph** before adding the descriptive elements.

------

## Why This Step Matters

Restoring phrases allows us to view the paragraph **in its full form** while still preserving the structural insights we have gained.

The paragraph now appears complete, yet its structure remains clearly visible.

This prepares us for the final step of the method.

# Lesson 9 — Step 8: Identify the Paragraph Spine

## What Is the Paragraph Spine?

After the structure of the paragraph has been revealed, the next step is to identify the **paragraph spine**.

The paragraph spine is the **main line of thought** that runs through the paragraph.

It consists of the **primary clauses** that carry the author's central statements. All other clauses and phrases exist to support, explain, or qualify these main ideas.

When the supporting material is temporarily set aside, the spine becomes visible.

------

## Why the Spine Matters

The spine reveals the **core message** of the paragraph.

Once we see the spine, we can understand what the author is **primarily asserting**.

All other parts of the paragraph help us understand or expand that central assertion.

In many cases, the author's purpose becomes clear almost immediately once the spine is identified.

------

## How to Find the Spine

To identify the paragraph spine, ask the following questions:

- Which clauses can stand on their own as statements?
- Which clauses are being explained by other clauses?
- Which clauses remain if the supporting material is removed?

The clauses that remain form the **spine of the paragraph**.

------

## Example

Consider the structured paragraph developed in the previous lessons:

```
[STATEMENT]
And I, brothers, could not ==speak== to you
    as to spiritual men
    (but → CONTRAST) as to men of flesh
        as to infants in Christ

[STATEMENT]
I ==gave== you milk to drink
    not solid food
    (for → REASON) you ==were== not yet able
```

When the supporting material is temporarily removed, the main statements become easier to see:

```
I could not speak to you

I gave you milk to drink
```

These two statements form the **paragraph spine**.

Everything else in the paragraph explains or qualifies these statements.

------

## What Students Should Notice

Once the spine is visible, students should observe:

- which statements carry the paragraph
- which clauses explain those statements
- how the supporting material strengthens the author's point

The spine provides a clear view of the author's **main assertions**.

------

## A Helpful Insight

Students often discover that the spine consists of **very simple statements**.

Although the paragraph itself may appear complex, the core message is often straightforward once the supporting clauses are removed.

This is why identifying the spine is such an important step.

------

## Why This Step Matters

Without identifying the paragraph spine, readers may focus on smaller details and miss the author's main point.

The spine allows students to see the **central structure** of the paragraph before attempting to state its purpose.

# Lesson 10 — Step 9: State the Author’s Purpose

## Why This Is the Final Step

All the previous steps have prepared us for this moment.

We have:

- identified the **finite verbs**
- located the **clause connectors**
- separated the **clauses**
- indented the **dependent clauses**
- restored the **phrases**
- identified the **paragraph spine**

Because the structure is now visible, we can determine what the author is **doing** in the paragraph.

This is called the **author's purpose**.

------

## What Is the Author’s Purpose?

The author's purpose describes **the function of the paragraph**.

In other words:

**Why did the author write these statements together?**

The answer should describe **what the author is accomplishing**, not merely what the paragraph contains.

For example, an author may be:

- explaining something
- correcting something
- contrasting two realities
- giving instructions
- defending a claim
- exposing a problem
- encouraging a response

The purpose is determined by observing **how the clauses work together**.

------

## Begin With the Spine

The easiest way to state the author's purpose is to begin with the **paragraph spine**.

Example spine:

```
I could not speak to you
I gave you milk to drink
```

Now we ask:

What is the author doing with these statements?

When we look at the supporting clauses, we see that the author is explaining **why this was necessary**.

This allows us to state the purpose.

------

## Example Purpose Statement

A clear purpose statement might look like this:

**Paul explains why he was forced to treat the Corinthians as immature believers.**

Notice several things about this statement.

It:

- describes **what Paul is doing**
- reflects the **main clauses of the paragraph**
- incorporates the **supporting explanation**

It does not speculate beyond what the paragraph itself reveals.

------

## A Helpful Check

A good purpose statement should satisfy three conditions.

It should:

1. Fit the **paragraph spine**
2. Account for the **supporting clauses**
3. Reflect the **overall movement of the paragraph**

If a purpose statement ignores the structure of the paragraph, it is probably inaccurate.

# The 10-Example Progression

## Example 1 — One Clause (No Structure Yet)

Goal:
 Students learn that **not every sentence is complex**.

Text
 1 Corinthians 2:2

> “For I determined to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and Him crucified.”

What students learn:

- Identify **finite verbs**
- Recognize **one clause**
- See how **phrases expand a clause**

Difficulty level: ⭐

------

# Example 2 — Two Main Clauses

Goal:
 Students learn to see **parallel statements**.

Text
 1 Corinthians 3:1

> “And I, brothers, could not speak to you as to spiritual men, but as to men of flesh, as to infants in Christ.”

What students learn:

- Identify **two contrasting ideas**
- Recognize **contrast connectors (but)**
- Distinguish **phrases vs clauses**

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐

------

# Example 3 — A Clause Explaining Another Clause

Goal:
 Students learn to identify **reason clauses**.

Text
 1 Corinthians 3:2

> “I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able to receive it.”

What students learn:

- **For** introduces explanation
- Supporting clauses **justify the main statement**

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐

------

# Example 4 — Multiple Explanations

Goal:
 Students learn to follow **chains of explanation**.

Text
 1 Corinthians 3:3

> “For you are still fleshly; for since there is jealousy and strife among you, are you not fleshly?”

What students learn:

- stacked explanations
- recognizing **evidence clauses**

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 5 — Purpose Clauses

Goal:
 Students learn to identify **purpose structure**.

Text
 Romans 12:2

> “Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may prove what the will of God is.”

What students learn:

- **so that** introduces purpose
- commands leading to results

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 6 — Condition Clauses

Goal:
 Students recognize **conditional reasoning**.

Text
 Romans 8:13

> “If you are living according to the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you are putting to death the deeds of the body, you will live.”

What students learn:

- **if / then logic**
- contrasting outcomes

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 7 — Comparison Clauses

Goal:
 Students identify **comparison structures**.

Text
 Romans 5:18

> “So then as through one transgression there resulted condemnation to all men, even so through one act of righteousness there resulted justification of life.”

What students learn:

- parallel argument structure
- theological reasoning built through comparison

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 8 — Argument Development

Goal:
 Students see **progressive argument flow**.

Text
 Galatians 3:24–25

> “Therefore the Law has become our tutor to lead us to Christ, so that we may be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a tutor.”

What students learn:

- **therefore**
- **but now**
- development of an argument

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 9 — Complex Explanatory Structure

Goal:
 Students handle **multi-layered reasoning**.

Text
 Ephesians 2:8–9

> “For by grace you have been saved through faith; and that not of yourselves, it is the gift of God; not as a result of works, so that no one may boast.”

What students learn:

- stacked explanations
- clarifications
- exclusions

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

------

# Example 10 — Full Paragraph Structure

Goal:
 Students apply the **entire method to a paragraph**.

Text
 1 Corinthians 1:26–29

This paragraph contains:

- explanations
- contrasts
- purpose
- argument development

Students now perform the **entire method independently**.

Difficulty level: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Why This Progression Works

This sequence gradually trains students to recognize:

1. verbs
2. clauses
3. clause relationships
4. logical connectors
5. argument structure

By Example 10, students can handle **almost any paragraph in the New Testament**.

# The Method at a Glance

### Step 1 — Identify Finite Verbs

Find the verbs that carry the action of the sentence.

These verbs anchor the clauses.

Without verbs there are no clauses.

------

### Step 2 — Locate Clause Connectors

Look for words that connect ideas.

Common connectors include:

- for
- but
- therefore
- because
- if
- so that
- since

These words signal relationships between clauses.

------

### Step 3 — List the Clauses

Each finite verb normally introduces a clause.

Write the clauses on separate lines.

This begins to reveal the structure of the paragraph.

------

### Step 4 — Indent Dependent Clauses

Clauses that explain or support another clause should be indented beneath it.

This allows the logical relationships to become visible.

------

### Step 5 — Label Clause Relationships

Identify how the clauses relate to one another.

Common relationships include:

- reason
- contrast
- explanation
- condition
- purpose
- result

These relationships explain **how the argument moves forward**.

------

### Step 6 — Add the Phrases

Restore the phrases that add detail to each clause.

Phrases expand the meaning of the clause but do not normally determine the structure.

------

### Step 7 — Identify the Paragraph Spine

Remove the supporting clauses temporarily.

The clauses that remain form the **paragraph spine**.

This is the main line of thought.

------

### Step 8 — State the Author’s Purpose

Using the spine and supporting structure, describe what the author is doing in the paragraph.

This statement should describe **the function of the paragraph**, not merely its topic.

------

# The Big Picture

The process can be summarized in three movements.

### Discover the Structure

1. Find verbs
2. Identify clauses
3. Indent relationships

------

### Restore the Detail

1. Add phrases
2. Label relationships

------

### Understand the Paragraph

1. Identify the spine
2. State the author's purpose

# Common Student Difficulties

## 1. Confusing Phrases With Clauses

Students often think any group of words is a clause.

Remind them:

**A clause requires a finite verb.**

Example:

```
to know Christ
```

This is a **phrase**, not a clause.

------

## 2. Overlooking Finite Verbs

Students frequently miss verbs such as:

- is
- are
- was
- were

Encourage them to **circle every verb first**.

------

## 3. Assuming Every Connector Introduces a Clause

Words like:

- but
- for
- except

sometimes introduce phrases rather than clauses.

Students must check:

**Is there a finite verb after the connector?**

------

## 4. Overcomplicating the Spine

Students often think the spine must be complex.

In reality, the spine is often **very simple**.

Example:

```
I gave you milk
```

The rest of the paragraph explains **why**.

# Instructor Strategy

Teachers should resist the urge to give answers immediately.

Instead ask students questions such as:

- Where is the verb?
- Does this phrase contain a verb?
- What clause explains this statement?

These questions guide students toward **discovering the structure themselves**.

# Final Exercise Strategy

When students return to the **master paragraph**, allow them to work **individually first**.

Then compare results as a group.

Most students will immediately notice:

> The paragraph that seemed confusing at first is now very clear.

This moment reinforces the value of the method.
