# First Foundational Principle

Before tools, before exercises, before structure, the manual must define:

## What Is an Observation?

If this is not defined with precision, the entire project collapses.

Here is a working conceptual definition:

> An observation is anything that can be directly pointed to in the text and verified by another reader without requiring explanation.

Key elements:

- It must be visible.
- It must be demonstrable.
- It must not require inference.
- It must not require theological agreement.
- It must not require background knowledge.
- It must not require psychological assumptions.

If two students cannot independently identify it, it is not yet observation.

------

# Second Foundational Principle

## Observation is Description, Not Explanation.

Example:

Observation:

- The word “grace” appears three times.
- The author shifts from “they” to “you.”
- The verb is in past tense.
- A contrast is introduced by “but.”

Interpretation:

- The author emphasizes grace.
- He is correcting them.
- This is about justification.
- This means salvation is secure.

Those may be correct — but they are not observation.

Your manual must train students to feel that difference.

------

# Third Foundational Principle

## Observation Must Be Constrained

Observation becomes disguised interpretation when it:

- Adds motive (“Paul is angry”)
- Adds theology (“This teaches eternal security”)
- Adds assumed purpose (“He is comforting them”)
- Imports frameworks (“Covenantal shift here”)

The manual must explicitly train students to ask:

> “Can I show this directly from the wording alone?”

If not, it is premature.
