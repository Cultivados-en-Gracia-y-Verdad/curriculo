This is exactly the kind of "mega view" that can be observed without imposing themes.

Rather than asking "What is Titus about?", ask:

Where does pressure appear, develop, and resolve?

Following only the text, I see something like this.

Passage	Pressure	Development
1:1–4	None yet	Stable introduction. God's promise, truth, eternal life.
1:5	Disorder	Titus is left in Crete because something is unfinished.
1:6–9	Leadership pressure	Elders must withstand internal instability by holding the faithful word.
1:10–16	False teachers	Pressure becomes explicit. Empty talkers, deceivers, upsetting whole households.
2:1–10	Community pressure	Every group in the church must live differently because false teaching affects the whole household.
2:11–14	Divine answer	The grace of God appears and begins training God's people. Pressure is answered by God's action, not merely human effort.
3:1–2	Public pressure	Relationship with rulers and society. Christians live differently in the world.
3:3	Past pressure	Reminder: "we also once were..." The church once belonged to the same broken condition.
3:4–7	Divine intervention	God's kindness appears. Salvation is entirely God's work.
3:8	Productive pressure	Believers are urged toward good works.
3:9–11	Ongoing threat	Foolish controversies and divisive people remain dangers.
3:12–15	Resolution	Partnership, provision, fruitfulness, final greetings.
Watching the pressure rise

Notice the escalation.

Stage 1 — Something is unfinished (1:5)

No conflict has been named.

Only this:

"I left you in Crete..."

The pressure begins as unfinished work.

Stage 2 — Dangerous people appear (1:10–16)

Now the problem receives names.

rebellious
empty talkers
deceivers
upsetting households
teaching for dishonest gain
detestable
disobedient

Pressure has become visible.

Stage 3 — The church must respond (2:1–10)

Instead of discussing the false teachers further, Paul turns to:

older men
older women
younger women
younger men
slaves

Pressure spreads into every relationship.

This is interesting.

Paul doesn't merely refute error.

He builds a healthy household.

Stage 4 — Why this is possible (2:11–14)

This is the first great turning point.

Pressure is no longer answered primarily by instruction.

Instead,

The grace of God has appeared.

Then observe the verbs.

Grace...

brings salvation
trains
teaches
causes us to deny ungodliness
produces eager people for good works

The solution enters history.

Stage 5 — Society (3:1–2)

Pressure expands beyond the church.

Believers now interact with

rulers
authorities
everyone

The household lives under public pressure.

Stage 6 — Remember who we were (3:3)

Paul intentionally increases humility.

"We also once were..."

Now pressure becomes personal.

No believer stands above the world.

Stage 7 — God's kindness appears (3:4–7)

Notice something beautiful.

Chapter 2:

The grace of God appeared.

Chapter 3:

The kindness and love of God our Savior appeared.

Both turning points begin with God appearing, not man improving.

Stage 8 — Final remaining pressures (3:8–11)

Only two pressures remain.

Continue in good works.
Reject the divisive person.

False teaching has been reduced to a manageable problem.

Stage 9 — Peaceful ending (3:12–15)

The letter ends with:

cooperation
hospitality
supplying needs
learning good works
bearing fruit
greetings
grace

The atmosphere is entirely different from chapter 1.

An interesting observation

One thing that stands out is that Paul almost never attacks the opponents directly.

The amount of space devoted to the opponents is surprisingly small.

Instead, most of Titus is devoted to building healthy people.

The pressure therefore shifts like this:

External pressure
        ↓
Internal formation
        ↓
Healthy community
        ↓
Visible good works

That movement feels objective from the text itself. Rather than ending with "the false teachers have been defeated," Titus ends with "our people must learn to engage in good works to meet pressing needs, so that they will not be unfruitful" (3:14). The final pressure is not the opponents—it is whether God's people become fruitful. That is a notable development across the letter.
