Yes. I’m familiar with both, and I’ll describe them as methods, not as theological systems.
Deductive method (top-down)
Starting point:
A framework, theme, doctrine, outline, or conclusion is assumed before close attention to the text.
Process:
Begin with a concept (e.g., a doctrine, theme, logical structure).
Read the text through that lens.
Select, organize, and explain details that support the prior conclusion.
Characteristics:
Moves from general → specific
Often asks: “How does this text support what we already know?”
Relies on categorization, synthesis, and harmonization
Frequently smooths tensions or apparent contradictions
Risk:
The text can become illustrative rather than determinative.
Grammar, aspect, repetition, and shifts in voice may be subordinated to the framework.
Inductive method (bottom-up)
Starting point:
The text itself, without assuming structure, meaning, or conclusion.
Process:
Observe the text carefully (words, grammar, repetition, contrasts, subjects, verbs).
Allow patterns to emerge from the text.
Only afterward describe what the text appears to be doing or claiming.
Characteristics:
Moves from specific → general
Asks: “What is here?” before “What does this mean?”
Prioritizes:
Original-language grammar
Repetition and variation
Shifts in tense, voice, subject, object
Boundaries marked by the text itself
Leaves unresolved tensions visible if the text leaves them unresolved
Strength:
The text governs the conclusions, not the reverse.
Key contrast in one sentence
Deductive: Meaning is brought to the text.
Inductive: Meaning is allowed to arise from the text.
Important clarification (especially relevant to your work)
Inductive study does not mean:
No conclusions ever
No grammar decisions
No description of meaning
It means:
Conclusions are descriptive before explanatory
Grammar is used to establish what the text says, not what it must mean theologically
Interpretation is restrained until observation is exhausted
