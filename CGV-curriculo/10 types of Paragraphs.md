10 types of Paragraphs:

1. Descriptive
2. Informative 
3. Narrative 
4. Process (steps required to achieve a task)
5. Compare & contraste (similarities and differences between two things)
6. Critical (evaluate subject or event)
7. Persuasive  (aim to persuade reader to do something or perspective) 
8. Argumentative (logical argument to reader)
9. Problem/solution (problem and its solution)
10. Cause & effect (action and reaction)

11. Command 

## PARAGRAPH TYPES

1. Declaration (states reality, truth, fact) assertions, indicative verbs, identity statemetns
2. Explanation (explains something already stated) *for, because, since, in that*, explaining phrases
3. Argument/proof (defends or supports a claim) *therefore, if...then,* rhetorical questions, contrasting alternatives
4. Contrast (creates separation between two realities) *but, however, yet, rather, instead*
5. Condition (sets conditions or testing statements) *if, whoever, anyone who, when*
6. Conclusion (draws from prior development) *therefore, so then, thus, for this reason*
7. Command/Exhortation (calls for response) *let us, therefore...walk, command forms, imperatives*
8. Warning ()
9. Ground (therefore)

## SENTENCES

**Addition** (and, also, furthermore) It moves sideways. Adds another piece.

**Clarification** (which, that which) it describes, defines, or specifies something already stated. That explains what “present your bodies” amounts to.  It does not give a reason. It clarifies meaning.

**Contrast** (but, yet, however) It turns the direction.

# Paragraph Breaks

IF: 

The grammatical type changes

The subject focus changes

The development shifts direction



Detecting Development direction:

Activity = what the sentence is doing grammatically.



When you are unsure whether development shifts, ask three dumb questions:

1. Is the grammatical formula repeating?
2. Did the speaker role change?
3. Did the sentence activity change (testing, declaring, explaining, commanding, assuring)?

Now here’s the stabilizing sentence you were unable to write earlier:

Divide where the activity of the text changes.

That is your one-line rule.

Not:

Divide where the theme changes.

Not:

Divide where it feels different.

But:

Divide where the grammatical activity shifts.





How to start: 

If you train students to circle:

- **if** → conditional activity
- **but / yet** → contrast activity
- **for / because** → explanation activity
- **is / are** → declaration activity

Then before they ever talk about meaning, they will see activity patterns.
