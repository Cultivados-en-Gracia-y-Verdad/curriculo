# ROOTS Philosophy

## Purpose

ROOTS exists to help ordinary Bible readers observe the structure of the biblical text clearly, carefully, contextually, and as objectively as reasonably possible.

The goal is not to turn students into grammarians.

The goal is to empower them to slow down, look closely at the original-language text with appropriate morphological help, and listen to what the text is actually saying before deciding what they think it is saying.

ROOTS should transform the way people read Scripture by giving them a repeatable way to see what is there.

---

## 1. The Text Gets the First Word

Readers naturally bring memories, assumptions, theology, traditions, expectations, and familiar translations to a passage.

ROOTS does not pretend that anyone can become perfectly objective. Instead, it creates **objective friction against our assumptions**.

Before asking:

> What do I think this means?

ROOTS teaches the reader to ask:

> What is actually here?

> What is being said?

> Who or what is it about?

> What is being said about them or it?

> What belongs together?

> What connects to what?

> What carries the main thought, and what supports it?

ROOTS is therefore a method of disciplined listening.

---

## 2. Observation Before Interpretation

ROOTS begins with observation.

Interpretation matters, but interpretation should be built on careful observation rather than replacing it.

The reader should first discover as much as possible about the structure of the text itself:

- what is being said;
- where one statement begins and ends;
- what material belongs together;
- how statements connect;
- which ideas carry other ideas;
- how the flow of thought develops.

Only then is the reader better prepared to interpret what the passage means.

**Structure before interpretation.**

---

## 3. Original-Language Priority

ROOTS derives its structural observations from the original Greek, Hebrew, or Aramaic text.

Translations may later help with understanding and presentation, but they must not determine the structure.

The direction is:

**ORIGINAL LANGUAGE  
→ observation  
→ structure  
→ interpretation**

Never the reverse.

The student does not need to know Greek, Hebrew, or Aramaic grammar in the traditional sense. Morphological information can identify useful forms for the student.

Morphology tells the student what a form is.

ROOTS tells the student what to notice and what to do with that information.

---

## 4. Learn From Tradition Without Being Trapped by It

ROOTS should learn deeply from the history of grammatical study, linguistic research, traditional language instruction, discourse analysis, and biblical scholarship.

But ROOTS is not required to reproduce the traditional learning process.

A grammatical distinction may be true and useful for scholars while still being unnecessary—or even harmful—for a beginner trying to observe Scripture.

We therefore ask of every traditional category:

> Does the ROOTS student actually need this distinction in order to make the right observation?

If not, it can remain behind the scenes.

Tradition and scholarship help us **build and test ROOTS**.

They do not automatically determine how ROOTS must be taught.

---

## 5. Observation Language, Not School-Grammar Language

Many people approach grammar with years of frustration, embarrassment, confusion, or discouragement.

ROOTS should not unnecessarily reactivate those barriers.

Its student-facing language should feel like **looking, noticing, connecting, and listening** rather than taking a grammar class.

Instead of beginning with technical questions such as:

> Identify the subject and predicate.

ROOTS may ask:

> Who or what are we talking about?

> What is being said about them or it?

Instead of teaching technical terminology first, ROOTS should first teach the observation.

Terminology should be introduced only when it genuinely helps the student.

---

## 6. Two Languages of ROOTS

ROOTS should deliberately maintain two levels of language.

### ROOTS Research Language

Researchers may use precise linguistic terms such as:

- predication;
- predicate;
- clause;
- finite verb;
- participle;
- infinitive;
- subordination;
- coordination;
- syntax;
- discourse relationship.

This language helps us research, test, falsify, and defend the method.

### ROOTS Observation Language

Students should receive fresh, ordinary questions whenever possible:

- Who or what?
- What is being said?
- Is another thought starting here?
- What belongs with this?
- Is this carrying the thought or helping it?
- What is this connected to?
- Is the text giving a reason, contrast, condition, result, or something else?

The research layer may be technically sophisticated.

The student experience should remain as simple as the observation allows.

---

## 7. Grammar Serves Observation

ROOTS does not reject grammar.

ROOTS puts grammar in its proper place.

Grammar, morphology, and linguistic scholarship are tools that help us determine what observations are legitimate.

They are not the final educational goal.

A student should not have to become a Greek, Hebrew, or Aramaic grammarian in order to observe the biblical text responsibly.

The governing question is:

> What is the smallest amount of grammatical information the student must act on to make the correct structural observation?

---

## 8. Complexity Must Earn Its Place

ROOTS should never add complexity merely because a grammatical construction exists.

Every proposed rule must earn its place.

Before adding a rule, ask:

- What real observation does this rule recover?
- Does the simpler procedure systematically fail without it?
- Can a beginner actually perform it?
- Is the evidence visible in the text or morphology?
- How many wrong observations will the rule create?
- Can a simpler question accomplish nearly the same thing?
- Would uncertainty be better than teaching substantial extra grammar?

ROOTS should prefer a small, reliable procedure over grammatical completeness.

Sometimes:

`P?`

may be better than a page of grammar instruction.

---

## 9. Evidence Before Intuition

ROOTS should train students to point to evidence.

The goal is for the student increasingly to be able to say:

> I marked this because I can see this in the text.

rather than:

> It just feels like this is what the verse means.

ROOTS will never remove every judgment call, but it should reduce unnecessary intuition wherever observable evidence can replace it.

---

## 10. One Method Across the Biblical Languages

ROOTS is one method.

There should not be a separate Greek ROOTS, Hebrew ROOTS, and Aramaic ROOTS.

ROOTS asks the same observational questions of every biblical text.

The languages may supply different evidence for answering those questions.

Therefore:

> **ROOTS is language-independent in its questions and language-sensitive in its evidence.**

---

## 11. The Student Should Be Doing the Seeing

ROOTS should not merely give students expert conclusions.

It should give them a process they can actually perform.

The student should increasingly be able to discover the structure rather than being told what the structure is.

That means ROOTS rules should be:

- simple;
- observable;
- repeatable;
- morphology-assisted;
- beginner-usable;
- as objective as reasonably possible;
- linguistically defensible.

The expert work belongs behind the method so that the student can do the observing in front of it.

---

## 12. ROOTS Is Training in Listening

At its deepest level, ROOTS is not about grammar terminology or diagramming.

It is about learning to listen carefully to a text.

The reader slows down.

The reader notices.

The reader resists filling the passage with assumptions.

The reader follows the actual flow of what is being said.

The hope is that ROOTS produces readers who are better prepared to hear Scripture clearly because they have learned to observe before they conclude.

---

# Governing Design Principle

> **Use all the linguistic sophistication necessary to discover and defend the observation, but require from the student only the simplest reliable action needed to see it.**

And beneath that:

> **ROOTS should feel like learning to notice the text, not learning to pass a grammar class.**
